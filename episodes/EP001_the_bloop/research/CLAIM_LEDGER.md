### C02 — Established fact
The recording was made by an autonomous hydrophone array that NOAA had kept in the eastern equatorial Pacific since May 1996 to monitor seafloor earthquakes and volcanic activity.
- **SRC-005**: Monitoring Pacific Ocean seismicity from an autonomous hydrophone array (Christopher G. Fox, Haruyoshi Matsumoto, Tai-Kwan Andy Lau (NOAA PMEL / Oregon State University), 2001-03-10)
- **SRC-004**: What is the bloop? (NOAA National Ocean Service (Ocean Facts), unknown first publication; page shows last updated 2026-09-23)
- *Approved wording (draft):* "The microphones were not listening for monsters. They were there to hear earthquakes and eruptions on the seafloor."

### C03 — Established fact
The array was designed by NOAA's Pacific Marine Environmental Laboratory (PMEL) to record low-frequency sound in the SOFAR channel, producing results comparable to the U.S. Navy's SOSUS network, which NOAA scientists had also used for research.
- **SRC-005**: Monitoring Pacific Ocean seismicity from an autonomous hydrophone array (Christopher G. Fox, Haruyoshi Matsumoto, Tai-Kwan Andy Lau (NOAA PMEL / Oregon State University), 2001-03-10)
- **SRC-001**: A Collection of Sounds from the Sea (Andra M. Bobbitt (NOAA VENTS Program) and Sharon Nieukirk (Acoustic Monitoring Project), NOAA Pacific Marine Environmental Laboratory, via NOAA Ocean Explorer, unknown (undated page; Sound in the Sea 2001 expedition site))
- *Counter-evidence / caveats:* Popular accounts often say the Bloop was heard by Cold War submarine-hunting SOSUS hydrophones. NOAA's own page and Fox et al. (2001) identify the Equatorial Pacific autonomous array, a separate civilian system modelled on SOSUS results.
- *Approved wording (draft):* "NOAA's moored hydrophones hung in the deep sound channel, the layer the U.S. Navy had once used to track submarines."

### C20 — Strong evidence
The most likely cause of the Bloop is an icequake from a large Antarctic iceberg.
- **SRC-002**: Icequakes (Bloop) (NOAA Pacific Marine Environmental Laboratory, Acoustics Program, unknown (undated page; refers to events of early 2008))
- **SRC-009**: The Bloop mystery has been solved: it was never a giant sea monster (Ian Steadman, WIRED UK, 2012-11-29)
- **SRC-004**: What is the bloop? (NOAA National Ocean Service (Ocean Facts), unknown first publication; page shows last updated 2026-09-23)
- *Counter-evidence / caveats:* The conclusion rests on (1) spectral similarity to later icequake recordings and (2) the arrival direction. No one observed the 1997 event, the specific iceberg was never identified, and we found no peer-reviewed paper analysing the 1997 signal itself. Icequake activity near the Antarctic Peninsula peaks in austral summer (SRC-011), while the Bloop was recorded in the northern summer (austral winter); this does not rule ice out, but it is worth stating honestly.
- *Alternatives:* Another cryogenic process, such as an iceberg grounding or rubbing (NOAA attributes 'Slow Down' and 'Julia' to groundings); An unknown biological source (no supporting evidence; see C42)
- *Approved wording (draft):* "That makes an Antarctic icequake the best-supported explanation. Not proven by a witness, but matched by the physics, the direction and the sound itself."

- *Approved wording (draft):* "For years people said it was never heard again. In a sense that's true. But sounds just like it are heard every year."

### C31 — Established fact
Popular retellings often say the Bloop was recorded only once, lasted about fifteen seconds, or was heard by every sensor; NOAA's description says it was recorded repeatedly, rose over about one minute, and was heard on multiple sensors.
- **SRC-001**: A Collection of Sounds from the Sea (Andra M. Bobbitt (NOAA VENTS Program) and Sharon Nieukirk (Acoustic Monitoring Project), NOAA Pacific Marine Environmental Laboratory, via NOAA Ocean Explorer, unknown (undated page; Sound in the Sea 2001 expedition site))
- **SRC-004**: What is the bloop? (NOAA National Ocean Service (Ocean Facts), unknown first publication; page shows last updated 2026-09-23)

- **SRC-029**: Meet the Bloop, the mysterious sound from the bottom of the Pacific Ocean (io9 / Gizmodo, 2012)
- *Approved wording (draft):* "Online, the story grew: heard only once, lasting fifteen seconds, picked up by every microphone. None of that matches NOAA's record."

### C32 — Strong evidence
Other mystery sounds from the same era were later attributed by NOAA to icebergs running aground near Antarctica: 'Slow Down' (19 May 1997) and 'Julia' (1 March 1999).

- **SRC-019**: Julia (NOAA Pacific Marine Environmental Laboratory, Acoustics Program, unknown)

### C33 — Established fact
Icequakes from fracturing icebergs are a prominent part of the ocean soundscape near the Antarctic Peninsula, with activity peaking in the austral summer.
- **SRC-011**: Sources and levels of ambient ocean sound near the Antarctic Peninsula (Robert P. Dziak, DelWayne R. Bohnenstiehl, Kathleen M. Stafford, Haruyoshi Matsumoto, Minkyu Park, Won Sang Lee, Matt J. Fowler, Tai-Kwan Lau, Joseph H. Haxel, David K. Mellinger, 2015-04)
- **SRC-030**: Watch and Listen to the Surprisingly Noisy Death of an Iceberg (Jane J. Lee, National Geographic, 2013-07-17)

### C34 — Established fact
Sounds from large icebergs have been detected independently at very long ranges, e.g. Ross Sea icebergs heard by seismic stations in French Polynesia (2000) and icebergs B15a and C19a heard about 10,000 km away (2008-2009).
- **SRC-013**: Long-range detection of hydroacoustic signals from large icebergs in the Ross Sea, Antarctica (Jacques Talandier, Olivier Hyvernaud, Emile A. Okal, Pierre-Franck Piserchia, 2002)
- **SRC-014**: Antarctic's siren call: the sound of icebergs (H. Matsumoto, R.P. Dziak, D. Bohnenstiehl, J. Tournadre, T-K Lau, M. Fowler, J. Haxel, M. Park, W. Lee (NOAA PMEL publication 4150), unknown (c. 2014; discusses 2008-2009 data))
- **SRC-010**: Life and Death Sounds of Iceberg A53a (Robert P. Dziak, Matthew J. Fowler, Haruyoshi Matsumoto, DelWayne R. Bohnenstiehl, Minkyu Park, Kyle Warren, Won Sang Lee, 2013-06)
- *Approved wording (draft):* "Other scientists, on other instruments, have heard Antarctic icebergs from as far as French Polynesia, and even across the equator."

### C35 — Strong evidence
Dziak's team went to the Antarctic Peninsula to study underwater volcanoes and earthquakes, and had recorded 5,000 to 6,000 icequakes by 2013.
- **SRC-030**: Watch and Listen to the Surprisingly Noisy Death of an Iceberg (Jane J. Lee, National Geographic, 2013-07-17)
- **SRC-011**: Sources and levels of ambient ocean sound near the Antarctic Peninsula (Robert P. Dziak, DelWayne R. Bohnenstiehl, Kathleen M. Stafford, Haruyoshi Matsumoto, Minkyu Park, Won Sang Lee, Matt J. Fowler, Tai-Kwan Lau, Joseph H. Haxel, David K. Mellinger, 2015-04)

### C36 — Strong evidence
At distant hydrophones, sounds from disintegrating icebergs can dominate frequencies below 100 Hz, the range used by baleen whales.
