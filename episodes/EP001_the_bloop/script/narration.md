## 2. The mysterious recording (context and anomaly)

Light fades quickly in the sea. Go deep enough, and the ocean is completely dark. But sound behaves differently.

[pause]

Deep in the ocean there is a layer where sound travels at its slowest, and that layer traps it. Inside this channel, a low rumble can cross an entire ocean basin.

[pause]

In 1944, two American scientists proved it with small explosive charges. A sound set off in this layer could be heard hundreds of miles away.

[pause]

During the Cold War, the U.S. Navy wired parts of the deep sea with microphones to hunt submarines. When the Cold War ended, scientists were allowed to listen too, and they heard volcanoes erupting on the seafloor. That success gave NOAA's scientists an idea: build listening stations of their own, in parts of the ocean the Navy's network did not cover.

[pause]

In May 1996, scientists at NOAA's Pacific Marine Environmental Laboratory began listening to that channel in the eastern equatorial Pacific, with a line of hydrophones moored in the deep. They were not hunting for monsters. They were listening for earthquakes and eruptions on the seafloor.

[pause]

Then, in the summer of 1997, the hydrophones caught something else.

[pause]

A powerful, low sound. Its pitch climbed steeply over about a minute. It wasn't a single event. NOAA recorded it again and again that summer. And it was loud enough to reach several hydrophones more than five thousand kilometres away.

[pause]

NOAA's first estimate placed its source in the remote South Pacific, roughly fifty degrees south and one hundred degrees west, far off the coast of southern South America.

[pause]

Now listen again, slowed back down. Sixteen times slower, the Bloop is not a bloop at all. It is a deep, rising groan, far below the pitch the clip made famous.

[pause]

Speeding a recording up sixteen times raises every pitch by four full octaves, and squeezes a minute of sound into less than four seconds. That is why the famous clip sounds short, round and strangely alive.

[pause]

So what made it?

[pause]

Earthquakes rumble. Ships hum. This was different. Its pitch changed quickly, the way animal calls do. In 2002, the scientist leading NOAA's acoustic monitoring work, Christopher Fox, told New Scientist that of all the unexplained sounds his team had collected, this was the one that most resembled an animal.

[pause]

But there was a problem with that idea. To be heard so far away, the source had to be enormous, or enormously efficient at making sound.

[pause]

NOAA's own list of guesses from that time reads like the start of a thriller: secret military exercises, ship engines, fishing-boat winches, giant squid, whales, an unknown creature. And by NOAA's estimate, around ninety-five percent of the ocean is unexplored. A sound with no source, from a place almost no one has seen. The mystery was born.

## 3. The investigation

Here is the part of the story most people never hear. Even in 2001, the team suspected an ordinary answer. Christopher Fox said publicly that they suspected ice, off the coast of Antarctica.

[pause]

But suspicion is not proof. In an official NOAA release that same year, Fox still listed the Bloop as a sound whose origin had not been determined. To be sure, the scientists needed to listen much closer to the ice.

[pause]

So NOAA went south. In December 2005, a team led by geophysicist Robert Dziak put hydrophones into the water off the Antarctic Peninsula, in the Bransfield Strait. Their arrays kept recording there, and in the Scotia Sea, until 2009.

[pause]

And once again, they had come to listen for volcanoes and earthquakes, and heard ice instead. Thousands of times.

[pause]

Around Antarctica, cracking ice turned out to be one of the most prominent sounds in the sea. Icebergs groan as they run aground. They shudder as they scrape the seafloor. And when they crack and fracture, they release bursts of sound called icequakes.

[pause]

In early 2008, the hydrophones followed one iceberg, known as A53a, about sixty kilometres long, as it broke apart near the island of South Georgia.

[pause]

Its icequakes left a familiar shape on the screen. Spectrograms very similar to the Bloop. And icequakes like these can be heard on hydrophones more than five thousand kilometres away.

[pause]

The Bloop also had company. Two other strange sounds from the same years, nicknamed Slow Down and Julia, were traced to icebergs running aground off Antarctica.

[pause]

There was one more clue. A sound reaches each hydrophone at a slightly different moment, and those tiny differences reveal the direction it came from. When NOAA traced the Bloop's arrival direction, the line did not stop in that empty patch of ocean. It pointed on, toward the edge of Antarctica, somewhere between the Bransfield Strait and the Ross Sea, or perhaps Cape Adare.

[long pause]

## 4. Scientific explanations (possibilities: the evidence for ice)

So what is an icequake? Large icebergs are not solid, silent blocks. As they drift, warm, collide and ground, they crack and fracture. Each break releases energy into the water as sound. Low-frequency sound loses little energy in the deep sound channel, so a big enough event can carry across an ocean.

[pause]

And this isn't rare. In 2012, Robert Dziak said that every year there are tens of thousands of icequakes from cracking sea ice and ice breaking off glaciers. Asked about the Bloop directly, he said its frequency and duration were essentially identical to icequakes his team had recorded off Antarctica.

[pause]

NOAA's laboratory put its conclusion carefully. The sounds recorded in 1997 are consistent with icequakes generated by large icebergs as they crack and fracture.

[pause]

Notice what that sentence does not say. It does not name an iceberg. It does not give a precise location. And it does not say the iceberg broke away from a glacier, although some retellings do.

[pause]

Other scientists, using other instruments, have heard Antarctic icebergs from astonishing distances. Seismic stations in French Polynesia picked up icebergs drifting in the Ross Sea. Hydrophones have heard two giant icebergs breaking apart from about ten thousand kilometres away, across the equator. At times, breaking ice even dominates the low frequencies that great whales use to call to each other.

[pause]

Could it have been a different kind of ice event, such as an iceberg scraping the seafloor? That fits the same family of sounds, and NOAA has linked other signals to groundings. But for the Bloop itself, NOAA points to icequakes.

[long pause]

## 5. Popular theories (possibilities: what people believed)

While scientists quietly gathered evidence, the public story went somewhere else entirely.

[pause]

By 2002, news reports were asking whether a giant creature was hiding in the deep, perhaps a giant squid. Scientists said "unknown". Many readers heard "monster".

[pause]

The most repeated claim was that the Bloop was several times louder than a blue whale, the loudest known animal. That line has been widely quoted as coming from NOAA, but we could not find it in NOAA's current pages, and we found no published measurement of how loud the Bloop actually was at its source. The comparison came from how far it travelled.

[pause]

For scale, blue whales really are among the loudest animals alive. Off Antarctica, scientists measured their calls at around 189 underwater decibels, and located calling whales up to two hundred kilometres away. Underwater decibels are not the same as decibels in air, so that number cannot be compared with a jet engine or a rock concert. What we can say is this: a sound heard more than five thousand kilometres away needed no whale at all, only a very large source of energy, like breaking ice.

[pause]

There is no evidence for a giant creature. The idea survives partly because the sped-up recording sounds alive.

[pause]

Then there is Cthulhu. In a horror story published in 1928, H. P. Lovecraft placed his sunken city, R'lyeh, at forty-seven degrees south, one hundred and twenty-six degrees west. Online, the Bloop was said to come from almost exactly there.

[pause]

The Bloop's first estimated position was nearly two thousand kilometres from Lovecraft's fictional coordinates. On a world map that looks close. In the ocean, it is not. And the later direction pointed even farther away, toward Antarctica.

[pause]

In 2012, a television film presented as a documentary, Mermaids: The Body Found, used the Bloop as part of its "evidence" for mermaids. It was fiction. So many viewers asked questions that NOAA issued a statement: no evidence of aquatic humanoids has ever been found.

[pause]

And the theory of a secret military test? Fox did not believe the sound was man-made, and there is no evidence that it was.

[pause]

Along the way, the story itself kept changing. It was heard only once. It lasted fifteen seconds. Every microphone picked it up. None of that matches NOAA's own record: recorded repeatedly, rising over about a minute, heard on multiple sensors. For years people said it was never heard again. In a sense that is true: that particular event never came back. But sounds just like it are recorded every year.

## 6. What the evidence actually tells us (investigation summary)

So, after all of it, what do we actually know?

[pause]

We know the sound was real, recorded repeatedly by NOAA in 1997. We know the famous clip is sped up, and that the real sound is a slow, deep rise lasting about a minute. We know its shape matches icequakes recorded near Antarctica, and that its arrival direction points toward the Antarctic coast. We know icequakes are common, and loud enough to cross an ocean.

[pause]

And we should be honest about what we do not know. No one saw the event. The iceberg was never identified. We found no scientific paper that analyses the 1997 recording itself; the conclusion lives on NOAA's web pages and in interviews. NOAA says the Bloop was recorded in summer. On an American agency's page, that most likely means the northern summer, which is winter in Antarctica, when icequakes there are less frequent. Less frequent, not absent. Even NOAA's own pages disagree about when the case was closed: 2005, 2008, or 2012, depending on where you look.

[long pause]

## 7. Final verdict (verdict and closing question)

Here is our verdict. The Bloop was almost certainly not a creature. The best-supported explanation, backed by NOAA's own scientists, is an Antarctic icequake: a giant iceberg cracking apart, its sound carried thousands of kilometres by the ocean's deep sound channel. That conclusion is strongly supported by the sound itself, by its direction, and by everything heard near Antarctica since. But it was never witnessed, and it never will be.

[pause]

Maybe that is the real lesson of the Bloop. The mystery was never about a monster. It was about how little we had been listening.

[pause]

NOAA's hydrophones still listen to the ocean, and its scientists have always expected that some of what they hear will remain a mystery. The question is no longer what made the Bloop. It is what else the ocean is telling us, that we haven't learned to hear.
