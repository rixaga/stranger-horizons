## 2. The mysterious recording (context and anomaly)

Light fades quickly in the sea. Go deep enough, and the ocean is completely dark. But sound behaves differently.

[pause]

Deep in the ocean there is a layer where sound travels at its slowest, and that layer traps it. Inside it, a low rumble can cross an entire ocean basin.

[pause]

During the Cold War, the U.S. Navy wired parts of the deep sea with microphones to hunt submarines. When the Cold War ended, scientists were allowed to listen too, and they heard volcanoes erupting on the seafloor. So NOAA's Pacific Marine Environmental Laboratory built listening stations of its own. In May 1996, it moored a line of hydrophones in the eastern equatorial Pacific, to hear earthquakes and eruptions on the seafloor.

[pause]

Then, in the summer of 1997, the hydrophones caught something else.

[pause]

A powerful, low sound. NOAA described its pitch as rising rapidly, over about a minute. It came again and again that summer. And it was loud enough to reach several hydrophones more than five thousand kilometres away. The version you heard has been sped up sixteen times, so that human ears can hear it at all.

[pause]

NOAA's first estimate placed its source in the remote South Pacific, roughly fifty degrees south and one hundred degrees west, far off the coast of southern South America.

[pause]

Earthquakes rumble. Ships hum. This was different. Its pitch changed quickly, the way animal calls do. In 2002, the scientist leading NOAA's acoustic monitoring work, Christopher Fox, told New Scientist that of all the unexplained sounds his team had collected, this was the one that most resembled an animal.

[pause]

But to be heard so far away, the source had to be enormous, or enormously efficient at making sound.

[pause]

NOAA's own list of guesses from that time reads like the start of a thriller: secret military exercises, ship engines, fishing-boat winches, giant squid, whales, an unknown creature. And by NOAA's estimate, around ninety-five percent of the ocean is unexplored. A sound with no source, from a place almost no one has seen. The theories wrote themselves.

## 3. The theories (possibilities: what people believed)

Theory one: the giant. By 2002, news reports were asking whether a giant creature was hiding in the deep, perhaps a giant squid. The most repeated line was that the Bloop was several times louder than a blue whale, the loudest known animal. If that were true, the reasoning went, whatever made it had to be bigger than anything alive. In 2003, cryptozoologist Loren Coleman wrote that it most likely came from a marine beast. Scientists said "unknown". Many readers heard "monster".

[pause]

Theory two: the survivor. Others pictured something older: megalodon, a giant prehistoric shark, still surviving in the deep where no one looks. To believers, the Bloop was its call, finally caught on tape.

[pause]

Theory three: the sleeper. Then there is Cthulhu. In a horror story published in 1928, H. P. Lovecraft described a sunken city called R'lyeh, where a monstrous god lies dreaming beneath the South Pacific. He even gave its coordinates: forty-seven degrees south, one hundred and twenty-six degrees west. Seventy years later, a mysterious sound rose out of the same ocean. Online, the Bloop was said to come from almost exactly there. For some it was a joke. For others it was too perfect to be a coincidence. Hold on to that coordinate. We will come back to it.

[pause]

Theory four: the Navy knew. Remember who built the ocean's first great listening network. The Navy. In a NOAA press release from 2001, the agency's own scientist was quoted saying this kind of sound had been reported for decades by Navy sonar operators. Decades. Before anyone had heard of the Bloop. Some retellings go further. They claim the Navy was asked directly, and said it wasn't theirs. To a suspicious mind, that is exactly what you would say about a secret weapon, or a test you could not admit.

[pause]

Theory five: something not from here. In UFO circles, the Bloop became one more "unidentified submerged object". An alien craft, a hidden base on the seafloor, something using the deep ocean the way we use the sky.

[pause]

Theory six: the mermaids. In 2012, a television film presented as a documentary, Mermaids: The Body Found, used the Bloop as part of its evidence for a hidden species of sea people. It was fiction, with a disclaimer. But enough viewers believed it that the questions reached the U.S. government.

[pause]

And finally: the legend. Online, the Bloop grew a body. It got fan-made creature designs, invented expedition stories, and animated battles against other sea monsters. The story itself kept changing as it spread. It was heard only once. It lasted fifteen seconds. Every microphone picked it up. It was never heard again. Even today, some articles still call it unsolved.

[pause]

A giant. A survivor. A sleeping god. A Navy secret. Visitors. Mermaids. Every one of these theories has believers. But only one explanation has evidence. And it was found by the same people who recorded the sound in the first place.

## 4. The investigation (the real research, revealed)

Here is the part of the story most people never hear. Even in 2001, the team suspected an ordinary answer. Christopher Fox said publicly that they suspected ice, off the coast of Antarctica.

[pause]

But suspicion is not proof. Officially, the Bloop's origin had still not been determined. To be sure, the scientists needed to listen much closer to the ice.

[pause]

So NOAA went south. In December 2005, a team led by geophysicist Robert Dziak put hydrophones into the water off the Antarctic Peninsula, in the Bransfield Strait. Their arrays kept recording there, and in the Scotia Sea, until 2009. Once again, they had come to listen for volcanoes and earthquakes. And once again, they heard ice. Thousands of times.

[pause]

Around Antarctica, cracking ice turned out to be one of the most prominent sounds in the sea. Icebergs groan as they run aground and scrape the seafloor. And when they crack and fracture, they release bursts of sound called icequakes.

[pause]

In early 2008, the hydrophones followed one iceberg, known as A53a, about sixty kilometres long, as it broke apart near the island of South Georgia.

[pause]

Its icequakes left a familiar shape on the screen. Spectrograms very similar to the Bloop. And icequakes like these can be heard on hydrophones more than five thousand kilometres away.

[pause]

There was one more clue. A sound reaches each hydrophone at a slightly different moment, and those tiny differences reveal the direction it came from. When NOAA traced the Bloop's arrival direction, the line did not stop in that empty patch of ocean. It pointed on, toward the edge of Antarctica, somewhere between the Bransfield Strait and the Ross Sea, or perhaps Cape Adare.

[long pause]

## 5. Scientific explanations (the sound of breaking ice)

So what is an icequake? Large icebergs are not solid, silent blocks. As they drift, collide and ground, they crack and fracture, and each break releases energy into the water as sound. Low-frequency sound loses little energy in the deep sound channel, so a big enough event can carry across an ocean.

[pause]

And it isn't rare. In 2012, Robert Dziak said that every year there are tens of thousands of icequakes. Asked about the Bloop directly, he said its frequency and duration were essentially identical to icequakes his team had recorded off Antarctica.

[pause]

NOAA's laboratory put its conclusion carefully. The sounds recorded in 1997 are consistent with icequakes generated by large icebergs as they crack and fracture.

[pause]

Now listen again, slowed back down to its real speed. Speeding a recording up sixteen times raises every pitch by four full octaves. NOAA's ten-second clip holds almost three minutes of the original recording. Most of the real sound sits so low that, at its true speed, it is barely audible to human ears. That short, round, strangely living "bloop" was never the voice of anything. It was a side effect of speeding up a deep, slow rumble so that we could hear it.

## 6. What the evidence actually tells us (investigation of every theory)

So let's go back through every theory.

[pause]

The giant. No published measurement shows the Bloop was "several times louder than a blue whale". That line came from how far the sound travelled, not from measuring it. For scale, scientists off Antarctica measured blue whale calls at around 189 underwater decibels, and located calling whales up to two hundred kilometres away. A sound heard more than five thousand kilometres away needed no animal at all, only a very large source of energy, like breaking ice.

[pause]

The survivor. Megalodon disappears from the fossil record around two and a half million years ago. There is no evidence that it survives.

[pause]

The sleeper. Remember that coordinate.

[pause]

The Bloop's first estimated position was nearly two thousand kilometres from Lovecraft's fictional city. On a world map that looks close. In the ocean, it is not. And NOAA's later direction pointed even farther away, toward Antarctica.

[pause]

The Navy. That 2001 quote is real, but nothing else we found supports decades of Navy reports of this sound, and we found no primary source for the story that the Navy was asked and denied it. Fox himself did not believe the sound was man-made, and there is no evidence that it was.

[pause]

The visitors. There is no evidence of any craft.

[pause]

The mermaids. After that broadcast, NOAA issued a public statement: no evidence of aquatic humanoids has ever been found.

[pause]

The legend. NOAA's own record says the sound was recorded repeatedly, rose over about a minute, and was heard on multiple sensors. And that particular event was never heard again, but sounds just like it are recorded every year.

[pause]

Now, the honest part. The ice explanation has limits too. No one saw the event. The iceberg was never identified. We found no scientific paper that analyses the 1997 recording itself; the conclusion lives on NOAA's web pages and in interviews. NOAA says the sound was recorded in summer. On an American agency's page, that most likely means the northern summer, which is winter in Antarctica, when icequakes there are less frequent. Less frequent, not absent. And even NOAA's own pages disagree about when the case was closed: 2005, 2008, or 2012.

## 7. Final verdict (verdict and closing question)

Here is our verdict. The Bloop was almost certainly not a creature, a weapon or a visitor. The best-supported explanation, from NOAA's own scientists, is an Antarctic icequake: a giant iceberg cracking apart, its sound carried thousands of kilometres by the ocean's deep sound channel. That conclusion is backed by the sound itself, by its direction, and by everything heard near Antarctica since. But it was never witnessed, and it never will be.

[pause]

Maybe that is the real lesson of the Bloop. The mystery was never about a monster. It was about how little we had been listening.

[pause]

NOAA's hydrophones still listen to the ocean, and its scientists have always expected that some of what they hear will remain a mystery. The question is no longer what made the Bloop. It is what else the ocean is telling us, that we haven't learned to hear.
